## Mental Model (read first)

This is a **finding aid**, not a transcription manual or the full interview collection. Its preface explains why retrospective personal accounts preserve information and relationships that written records may omit. Its entries help a researcher identify a narrator, locate materials, understand described topics and inspect recorded conditions of use. Those functions support faithful handling of a transcript, but neither a catalog entry nor an editor's summary can establish the precise words, order or completeness of the underlying interview.

Distinguish three layers throughout: **explicit statements** in the guide; **conventions observed in sampled entries**; and **this skill's editorial adaptations**. Do not promote the last two into rules the editors purportedly issued. The supplied OCR contains broken table cells and scanning noise; interpret damaged fields cautiously.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Preface — purpose, selection and retrospective testimony

**Locator:** printed pp. ix–x; supplied Markdown lines 360–455.

**Stated:** 72 women were interviewed between 1976 and 1981. Selection did not use scientific sampling or attempt to include every outstanding Black woman. The project sought a cross section of contributions to American society, including professional achievement, family work and voluntary service. These are the project's own scope statements, not grounds for claiming representativeness beyond them.

Interviewers cooperated with memoirists to explore family, education, work, voluntary activity and personal life. Narrators were invited to reflect on formative influences and historical events. The preface acknowledges that the method does not always yield a precise, objective or complete factual narrative. Its value includes fugitive information, relationships and circumstances absent from written records.

**Editorial consequence, synthesized for this skill:** a recollection can be valuable evidence of remembered experience without being verified evidence that every described event happened exactly that way. Retain perspective, uncertainty and emphasis in the record. Put factual questions or corroborating material in separate notes. Neither “the narrator said it” nor “an external source disagrees” alone establishes a transcription error.

**Boundary:** the guide does not furnish a modern ASR method, a confidence scale, a filler-deletion policy or an assessment of any particular automated transcript. It also does not justify treating every unusual grammatical form as speech-recognition damage.

### Preface — review, agreements and the recording

**Stated:** after transcription, each memoirist was given an opportunity to edit and correct her oral history before preparation of the final copy. Each signed an agreement stipulating terms of use. Tapes were preserved at the Schlesinger Library, and some recordings and transcripts were restricted at the time described. Most transcription and processing was complete by the end of 1985.

**What follows:** review was part of this project's process, and a signed agreement was distinct from the act of speaking or transcription. **What does not follow:** that all interview projects give narrators exclusive final authority, that every narrator exercised review, that a modern transcript has already been approved, or that factual accuracy follows from approval. The source says an opportunity was offered; it does not establish universal editorial jurisdiction.

**Adaptation:** preserve provenance and actual review state. If the task calls for review, distinguish submitted, edited, returned and approved versions. A narrator's later clarification may be recorded as a clarification rather than silently presented as the original utterance, depending on the project's agreed protocol. Do not use this guide to override that protocol.

### Note on the Guide — explicit limits and bracket usage

**Locator:** printed p. xv; supplied Markdown lines 596–645.

The stated audience comprises researchers, teachers and students, plus catalogers and other custodians. Each entry supplies a brief biography, a summary of primary topics and access information; notes on other research materials are explicitly incomplete. The index indexes **the guide**, not all contents of the interviews. Some transcripts were still being processed when the guide was compiled.

**Explicit bracket rule:** approximate or estimated information appears in square brackets. This applies to supplied information of uncertain precision; it is not a blanket instruction to invent estimates. No unsupported interview date should be supplied merely because it can be bracketed.

**Observed abbreviation convention:** DHEW is expanded using its historical name, followed by a bracketed current name in the guide's abbreviation list. This demonstrates one transparent historical-name update. It does not say that every term should be modernized, or that all square brackets in all transcripts must originate with a present editor.

**Adaptation:** bracket new editorial expansions or clarifications and preserve the original label where relevant. Identify existing source brackets rather than overwriting their provenance. The skill's broader bracket convention also draws on Einsohn and Schwartz's quotation treatment; it is not attributed wholly to this finding aid.

### Descriptive entries — identity, objects and retrieval

**Locator:** sampled entries for Margaret Walker Alexander (printed pp. 9–10), Kathleen Redding Adams (pp. 5–6), and the access block for Sadie Tanner Alexander; see source spans in the ledger.

The sampled entries distinguish the following information. This is a **descriptive schema inferred from the samples**, not a claim that every entry was audited or follows an identical complete template.

| Field | What it preserves | Editorial use and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Name and variants | Personal, married or professional names and life dates | Link verified aliases without flattening historically meaningful differences or assuming every similar name is the same person |
| Transcript and tape identifiers | Separate catalog identifiers for text and sound | Identify the object actually consulted; never imply a recording was heard because a transcript was read |
| Interviewer and interview date(s) | The encounter in which testimony was produced | Keep source-supplied session information; do not invent dates or participants |
| Copyright statement | The holder and year as cataloged | Preserve as a historical statement, not a present rights determination |
| Biography | An editor's contextual account | Do not merge the guide writer's biography into the narrator's spoken words |
| Interview contents | A selective topic summary | Useful for discovery, but not a verbatim transcript, exhaustive inventory or demonstrated chronological sequence |
| Suggested catalog entries | Subjects and other access points | Retrieval labels are catalog choices, not necessarily speaker vocabulary |
| Terms of use | Conditions separated by material and activity | A permission for one use does not establish another |
| Tape description | Masters, reels or listening copies as described | Material information, not an ASR quality rating |
| Other materials, photographs, catalog number | Links to related holdings | Incomplete leads, not a complete source bibliography |

**A consequential correction:** the previous reference inferred that topic strings followed the speaker's actual order. The guide only promises summaries of primary subjects; the inspected entries do not prove original sequence. The skill may keep headings in the **supplied transcript's observed order**, but cannot attribute this as a rule demonstrated by the guide.

### Terms of use — object and action must remain separate

The sampled entries separate research use, interlibrary loan, reproduction and quotation for publication. They also describe transcript access separately from recording access. Kathleen Adams's transcript is described as open for research while publication quotation and tape use have additional conditions; Margaret Walker Alexander's entry contains more restrictive transcript-use terms and closes the tape to research.

**Transferable distinction:** preserve what is restricted, for which action, and according to which record. “Available,” “open” and “approved” are not interchangeable statuses. A research-access statement cannot substitute for a publication decision.

**Currency boundary:** these are descriptions in a 1991 source, not current library policy or legal advice. Present permissions require the applicable current record and responsible authority. The skill should carry supplied conditions accurately and expose unknown status rather than invent clearance or silently impose the historic project's procedure on an unrelated interview.

### Index — discovery is not completeness

**Basis:** the guide's explicit note on index scope, rather than an exhaustive new index audit. A subject absent from the index may still occur in the full interviews. Likewise a topic mentioned in the guide does not identify all relevant passages or verify their wording.

For transcript editing, a summary, table of contents or entity list assists navigation; none is a substitute for checking the actual passage. An incomplete or unprocessed source should remain labeled as such. Do not report full interview coverage after inspecting only a finding aid.

## Worked Example

**Margaret Walker Alexander: keep the catalog's layers distinct.** The entry identifies Margaret Abigail (Walker) Alexander, gives Margaret Walker as her professional name, names the interviewer and dates, and distinguishes transcript and tape identifiers. Its biography and interview-content summary are catalog prose, not the words of the interview.

The entry separately records written permission for transcript use and loan, the director's permission for reproduction, joint conditions for publication quotation, and a tape closed to research use. A single “restricted” flag would lose which object and which activity each condition concerns; a single “transcript available” flag would lose even more.

Applied to a supplied interview, the editor would preserve verified aliases, session provenance and the relevant recorded terms beside the cleaned text. The editor could not claim to have listened to that tape, recover verbatim statements from the biography, infer the original order from the contents list, or declare publication permission from this historical entry. The example reconstructs the entry's information relationships; it does not reproduce an interview or grant access.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- When a narrator's recollection conflicts with external information, preserve the utterance and distinguish corroboration from transcription repair.
- When review status matters, report the actual state; an opportunity to review is not evidence of approval or universal final authority.
- When an approximate field is supplied, retain its visible uncertainty. Brackets do not warrant inventing a missing value.
- When adding a contemporary name, keep the historical form and identify the addition; do not attribute editorial context to the speaker.
- When two names may identify the same person, preserve the forms and establish the relationship before merging identities.
- When evaluating use conditions, inspect the material and the proposed action separately. Historic descriptions cannot determine present permissions.
- When the source is a guide or index, restrict coverage claims to that layer. Absence from a guide does not prove absence from the interview.
- When adding headings, use the observed transcript order; the guide supplies no verified order for an unseen transcript.

## Key Takeaways

1. Oral testimony preserves remembered perspective as well as information about events; those are different evidential functions.
2. This is a finding aid with selected descriptions, not the full transcripts or a transcription handbook.
3. Narrator review and terms of use are documented project practices with explicit limits of transfer.
4. Identity, source object, editorial additions and review state should remain distinguishable.
5. Access is described per object and action; old catalog statements need current verification for present use.
6. Sampled conventions and modern skill adaptations must remain labeled as inferences or adaptations.
