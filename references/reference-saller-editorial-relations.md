## Mental Model (read first)

Saller's subversion is a reordering of loyalties: the editor's first duty is not to the style manual, not to the writer, and not to their own standards, but **to the reader** — and the only practical route to the reader runs *through* the writer, whose cooperation you therefore cannot afford to lose. From that single inversion everything else follows. Most editorial conflicts are not disagreements about correctness at all; they are disagreements about **style, which is arbitrary and changeable**, mistaken for disagreements about **grammar, which mostly isn't**. The three working virtues are **carefulness, transparency, and flexibility**, and the hardest by far is the third. The book's counterweight to perfectionism: at some point the work has to be good enough, and you have to stop.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Introduction & Ch. 1 — The Subversive Copy Editor

- **Core idea**: Copyediting is defined not by job title but by activity — *"solving problems with writing is what copyediting is."* Anyone handling someone else's words is doing it.
- **Who's the boss?** — the deliberately provocative answer: not the style guide, not the paying institution, not the writer, and certainly not yourself. **First loyalty is to the reader**, the audience of the work being edited. This is presented as less radical than it sounds, since serving readers is the publisher's own mission.
- **Consequence — editing for the reader routinely means questioning established style rules.** The style appropriate to one subject is wrong for another; the same construction can be workaday jargon in one context and a joke in another; repetition can be necessary emphasis or mere noise. Rule-jettisoning in service of the reader is not a sacrifice of excellence but a condition of it.
- **The mantra for anyone new**: *"First, do no harm."*
- **Anti-pattern**: applying a rule consistently *because* it is a rule, without asking what it does for this document's reader.

### Ch. 2 — The Good Launch: Six Habits to Cultivate

- **Core idea**: The moment before you start editing is the moment of maximum goodwill — you haven't had a chance to do harm yet. Spend that capital on setup, not on speed.
- **1. Ask first, and ask nicely.** Questions before tinkering showcase competence, save rework, and reveal the writer's preferences and temperament. Their deeper purpose is to make the relationship **collaborative rather than adversarial** — so frame questions positively even when the manuscript is alarming. **Anti-pattern**: passive-aggressive queries that hint the writer is careless; Saller gives a mock example of a "so sorry I can't figure out your system" query and marks it as exactly what not to send.
- **2. Don't sneak (much).** *Stealth-editing* *(term)*: making changes with change-tracking turned off. Some silent changes are legitimate — those that would clutter a redlined printout without informing anyone (stray double spaces, hyphen removals that render illegibly in tracked markup). The rule is not "never silent" but **"silent, and disclosed"**: note the class of change at first instance, and keep a running list of silent-change types to hand over with the edited text.
- **3. Eliminate surprises.** Non-content shocks damage the relationship as much as intrusive editing does — unexpected responsibilities, unexpected timing, unexpected process. Surface them early.
- **4. Check in** as the project progresses, rather than surfacing only at delivery.
- **5. Keep it professional** when disagreement arises.
- **6. Say "Yes."** Where you can accommodate a request, do; where you can't, pass it on faithfully rather than refusing on someone else's behalf.
- **Why this works** — the payoff Saller names: the writer trusts you because you show no sign of the **editorial inflexibility that terrifies writers.**

### Ch. 3 — Carefulness, Transparency, Flexibility *(the book's central framework)*

- **Carefulness is actually knowledge.** Meticulousness without command of current style and grammar is worthless — you will read serenely past everything that should have set off an alarm. Her worked case: an editor who painstakingly inserted a thousand commas that the governing style manual doesn't call for. Care applied in ignorance produces confident, uniform error.
  - **How**: study the style manual gradually; the goal is *knowing what's in it and how to find it*, not memorizing it.
  - **The writer holds knowledge you don't** — two kinds specifically: the subject, and the knowledge level of the intended reader. Defer on both. But **"don't just shove everything into her lap"**: do your own part first, then hand over a *narrowed*, specific question showing what you already checked. Her worked query lists the exact competing variants observed and asks the author to resolve them against a glossary.
  - **Anti-pattern**: condescension and over-explanation. Explanations should be sparing; endless justification of your editing is tedious for the writer and only exposes your insecurity.
  - **Also usable**: your own long experience *as a reader* is legitimate evidence. Saller endorses citing it directly in queries — noting where you stumbled, or that a name hadn't been introduced yet — as a way of showing that a subtle change isn't gratuitous.
- **Transparency: the see-through document.** Tell the writer beforehand what you intend to do; ask before meddling in murky areas; mark changes visibly as you work; summarize decisions in a cover letter of editorial notes or a style sheet delivered with the manuscript. **Transparency happens before, during, and after**, and each stage is an invitation to participate. Its purpose is to make the edit a collaboration rather than presenting a *fait accompli*.
  - Working through an intermediary doesn't license excluding the writer; you may have to decide unilaterally to keep moving, but the habits still apply.
- **Flexibility: a style is just a style.** Named as the hardest of the three. When you have imposed a rule consistently and the writer resists, the instinct is to fight — and Saller reads the common phrasing *"my author insists on…"* as the tell of a battle of wills, with pride and a little bit of power at stake.
  - **The load-bearing distinction**: **style rules** — punctuation, capitalization, hyphenation, preferred spellings, citation conventions — **are by nature arbitrary and changeable.** **Grammar rules** are stricter and less negotiable; grammar evolves, but slower and against more resistance. **Failure to tell these apart is named as the single most common source of editorial grief.**
  - Competing style systems differ *for reasons*: a news style optimizes for mass accessibility, neutral tone, and low word count; children's educational publishers constrain vocabulary and sentence length by grade. A rule that serves one purpose is simply inapplicable to another.
  - Even Chicago's own guidance has always disclaimed fixity — describing its rules as made for the average case rather than possessing the force of law — and Saller lists edition-to-edition reversals to make the point concrete.
  - **Stet** *(term)*: Latin "let it stand" — what an author writes to reject your edit. Treat it as information, not defeat.

### Ch. 4 — The Difficult Author

- **Core idea**: Genuinely difficult authors — those who refuse changes and won't discuss them — are *rare*. Saller counts fewer than half a dozen in an entire career. Don't organize your practice around them.
- **Author's practice observation, not a measured generalization**: the *more* published the writer, the more tolerant and grateful they usually are — they already know what embarrassment editing prevents. The exception is the writer previously burned by bad editing.
- **The stance**: the task is not to diagnose the writer's insecurities but to produce the best result for the reader. When early signals suggest resistance, **escalate the precautions rather than the argument** — be extremely conservative in editing, limit expectations from the outset, and grant that the writer may understand their readership in ways you don't.
- **Anti-pattern**: treating a writer's resistance as a challenge to professional honor.

### Ch. 4 — Firmness without a power contest

**Locator:** pp. 35–36. Flexibility is not unconditional surrender. Accept harmless restorations and undo edits made only for eloquence; then explain the remaining reader problem precisely. Saller also describes enforcing genuinely required corrections within a publisher's authority. Her distinction is the merits of the issue versus wounded professional pride.

**Transcript adaptation:** the editor can document a serious problem and ask for a resolution without silently falsifying direct speech. The source's publisher-author relationship does not give an assistant unlimited authority over testimony. Nor does a preference for speaker voice require concealing a known factual inconsistency. State the disagreement and the kind of output being prepared.

### Ch. 5 — Dear Writers: protect the working copy

**Locator:** pp. 46–48. Once editing has begun, untracked changes to an old manuscript create a merging problem: the returned replacement omits intervening editorial work and hides the author's small changes. Track revisions or send clearly located amendments, identify who holds the working copy, and flag major new material early enough to reassess review and timing.

**How for serial transcripts:** retain the submitted chunk, the current edit and a clear list of user corrections. If a passage is resubmitted, compare it before replacing the current edit. A revised name can affect previous chunks, while a new argument is new content, not a mechanical correction. This application is the skill's synthesis, not an ASR procedure described by Saller.

### Ch. 6 — The Dangerous Manuscript

**Locator:** pp. 59–63. Danger comes from two directions: repetitive work encourages inattention; complicated work encourages haste or faulty automation. The book's sequence for tedious work is **automate, delegate, reevaluate**, with **accept your fate** as the fallback.

- **Automate:** seek a reusable technique for a necessary repetitive task; inspect whether the operation is actually uniform before applying it.
- **Delegate:** obtain appropriate agreement, define a bounded portion and retain oversight. Saller warns against passing off a substitute's work as one's own or losing control of electronic files. This is a description of an editorial option, not authorization to contact people or create agents.
- **Reevaluate:** ask whether anyone needs to do the task. Is the material wrong or confusing, or merely styled differently? This does **not** mean simply giving up because a task exceeds one's ability.
- **Accept your fate:** if the work is necessary and cannot be avoided, perform it attentively, potentially in smaller chunks. Exhausting the shortcuts does not make the obligation disappear.

**Anti-pattern:** turning an uncertain word-inference task into bulk substitution because it recurs. Repetition of a problem does not make the solution mechanical.

### Ch. 7 — Collision insurance: five ways to avoid the big crash

**Locator:** pp. 74–78. Preserve the five-part structure: **Labels and folders; Trial runs; Version control; Transparency; Backup.**

1. Keep originals distinct from working documents, with names that reveal their role.
2. Test tricky or hard-to-undo operations on a clearly marked copy. Test copies themselves can be mistaken for current work; distinguish them visibly and avoid saving them over the master.
3. Keep original, edited, author-reviewed and final states identifiable. Agree who owns the current working copy; simultaneous untracked edits create uncertainty even when both people are careful.
4. Make files and their state intelligible to the next person. Remove hidden production debris from the final copy without erasing the review history.
5. Keep recoverable backups. Versions stored only inside one file can disappear together with that file.

The chapter's mistaken search-and-replace examples show why fluency with a tool is part of editorial care. A valid word may be turned into a different valid word that a spellchecker accepts. A domain-sensitive term, a proper name and a substring are especially poor candidates for blind replacement. Modern application commands must be verified separately; the book's historical menu behavior is not a specification.

### Ch. 8 — The Living Deadline

**Locator:** pp. 86–89. **Lists** hold the next actions, **schedules** show future dependencies and handoffs, and **logs** preserve what was completed. They answer different questions; one cannot replace the others. Update promises and dependencies when the work changes, and warn the responsible person when a delivery is endangered.

**How for a long transcript:** record the processed span, the next span, unresolved identity questions and which user feedback has been incorporated. A completion log must not be mistaken for a plan, and a plan does not prove work was completed. The original describes human project coordination; reminders or messages require their own authorization.

### Chs. 9–10 — Cooperation, reliability and honest scope

**Locator:** pp. 92–94, 105–106. **Play nicely, and work through channels** is Saller's coordination rule. Name the concrete problem, timing and dependency without exaggeration or blame. Knowledge-sharing supports production; manipulating colleagues or promising on their behalf does not.

For freelance overruns, distinguish the learning cost of routine work from genuinely unusual demands and from unnecessary perfectionism. Report a threatened estimate before it becomes a surprise. The book's fee-allocation suggestions are context-specific professional advice, not a billing policy for this skill. The transferable judgment is to make scope and actual progress visible and not disguise incomplete work as finished.

### Ch. 11 — The Zen of Copyediting

- **Core idea**: The trait that makes someone want to edit — meticulousness, perfectionism — is also the trait that makes them unable to stop. **"There's no end to the amount of fussing you can do with a manuscript,"** but the resources are finite. *At some point it has to be good enough, and you have to stop.*
- **The cost is not only time.** Saller's worked case: a freelancer hired for a light edit returned heavily rewritten copy twice, under-billed both times out of embarrassment — **and had missed typos and misspellings while absorbed in the rewriting.** Over-editing doesn't merely cost extra; it degrades the edit you were actually asked for.
- **Working to rule** *(term)*: deliberately editing to a specified number of hours. Two project types exist — the kind that deserves no more than its estimate, and the kind that takes however long it takes — and the skill is telling them apart. **How**: ask the assigning editor directly ("if this runs over estimate, is that okay?"). The answer is rarely explicit, but the *emphasis* in the reply reveals which type you have. Then divide the budget across the document up front rather than discovering the shortfall at the end.
- **Anti-pattern**: unbillable perfectionism — reworking by hand what a later stage of production will do automatically anyway.

## Worked Example *(one for the whole book)*

**The pre-emptive cover-letter framing.** Rather than fighting the same battle at every instance of contested jargon, Saller sets the terms once, in the cover letter, before the writer sees a single edit. The framing she reproduces does four things in three sentences: it explains *why* she queries unusual word choices (she couldn't find dictionary support), it pre-authorizes the writer to overrule her on technical jargon by simply writing *stet*, it names the actual editorial interest at stake (readers who may also be confused, who might be helped by a gloss), and it explicitly assigns the decision to the writer — *it's your call*.

The construction is worth studying because it satisfies all three virtues simultaneously. It is **careful** — the editor has already checked a dictionary and is reporting a finding, not a hunch. It is **transparent** — the writer learns the pattern before encountering it, so no query arrives as an accusation. And it is **flexible** — an informed preference is expressed, and the deciding authority is handed to the person who actually knows the subject and the audience.

The lesson generalizes past cover letters: **when a disagreement is going to recur, resolve the *category* once rather than the *instances* repeatedly.** Handling it up front costs one paragraph; handling it instance by instance costs the relationship.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- Preserve a known working copy; merge revisions visibly instead of replacing edited text with an untracked older version.
- Before a global operation, run a trial on a distinct copy and inspect representative changes and exceptions.
- Reevaluate whether a repetitive task is necessary; if necessary and unavoidable, complete it rather than quietly dropping it.
- Keep next actions, future dependencies and completed work distinguishable.

- First loyalty is to the reader; the route to the reader runs through the writer, so protect that relationship as an editorial asset.
- Before objecting, classify: is this **style** (arbitrary, negotiable, defer freely) or **grammar** (stricter, defensible)? Most fights are the first mistaken for the second.
- On harmless style preferences, defer and record the choice. If a real reader problem or an agreed production requirement remains, explain it and resolve it openly; deference is not silent abandonment of correctness.
- Ask before you meddle; ask positively; ask *narrowly*, having done your own part first.
- Silent changes are permitted where tracked markup would obscure more than it reveals — but disclose the class of change at first instance and keep a list.
- When you'll make a change throughout, explain once at the first occurrence and apply it silently thereafter.
- Explain sparingly. If the reason is obvious, don't state it; endless justification reads as insecurity.
- Cite your own reader-experience as evidence for subtle changes — it's more persuasive than rule citation.
- Faced with a resistant writer, edit *more* conservatively, not more assertively.
- Decide early which kind of project you have — the one that gets its estimate, or the one that takes what it takes — and budget across the whole document accordingly.
- Stop when it's good enough. Over-editing costs accuracy, not just hours.
- When a disagreement will recur, settle the category once instead of the instances repeatedly.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The reader is the boss** — not the manual, not the institution, not the writer, not you.
2. **Carefulness, transparency, flexibility** — and carefulness means *knowledge*, not fastidiousness.
3. **Style is arbitrary; grammar mostly isn't.** Confusing the two is the top cause of editorial conflict.
4. **First, do no harm.**
5. **Don't stealth-edit** — silent where tracking would obscure, but always disclosed and listed.
6. **Ask first, narrowly, having done your part** — and defer to the writer on subject and audience.
7. **It has to be good enough at some point, and you have to stop.** Over-editing makes you miss real errors.
