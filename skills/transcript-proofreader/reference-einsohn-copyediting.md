# The Copyeditor's Handbook: A Guide for Book Publishing and Corporate Communications (4th ed., 2019) — Amy Einsohn and Marilyn Schwartz

**Format**: md (OCR of print) | **Pages**: ~513 | **Sections**: 15 chapters in 3 parts | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)

Copyediting is a craft of *calibrated restraint*, not of correction. The editor serves three constituencies at once — author, publisher, reader — and none of them is the editor. The governing question at every sentence is **"Is this acceptable as the author wrote it?"**, never "Would I have written it this way?" Everything else in the book — levels, triage, querying, style sheets — is machinery for deciding *how much* to intervene when time, money, and the author's patience are all finite. The single most transferable idea for transcript work: **the editor's job is to separate what gets fixed from what gets flagged**, and to keep a written record of both.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Part 1, Ch. 1 — What Copyeditors Do

- **Core idea**: The copyeditor is the author's second pair of eyes, serving author, publisher, and reader — never their own vanity. The professional mantra is *"It's not my manuscript."*
- **The 4 Cs** — **clarity, coherency, consistency, correctness**, all in service of the **"Cardinal C": communication**. Use as a triage lens: a change that serves none of the 4 Cs is probably taste, not correction.
- **The four commandments of copyediting** — (1) don't lose or damage the manuscript or muddle file versions; (2) don't introduce an error into text that was correct; (3) don't change the author's meaning; (4) don't miss a critical deadline. Their internal hierarchy matters: an act of *commission* is graver than one of *omission*, and substituting your preferred wording is a transgression while **changing meaning is a "mortal offense."**
- **Editorial judgment**: *(term)* the publishing profession's name for the editor's instinct about when to intervene, when to leave well enough alone, and when to hand the problem back to the author. It is explicitly not rule-following — nobody supervises the line-by-line calls.
- **Six principal tasks** — mechanical editing; correlating parts; language editing; content editing; permissions; markup.
  - **Mechanical editing** *(term)*: bringing copy into conformity with an editorial or **house style** — spelling, hyphenation, capitalization, punctuation, numbers, quotations, abbreviations, italics, special elements, documentation format. "There is nothing mechanical about mechanical editing." **Anti-pattern**: the classic novice error is to rewrite prose while ignoring "minor details" like capitalization and hyphenation. That inverts the priority.
  - **Language editing** *(term)*: grammar, usage, and diction. Its rules are far more subjective than mechanical ones; the "correct" choice shifts with house style, field conventions, and audience.
  - **Content editing**: flag serious internal inconsistencies, organizational problems, or missing apparatus — but with discretion, since interrupting the schedule for fundamental problems is rarely welcome.
- **Anti-pattern — ILF / ISF**: changing an author's wording because "**it looks funny**" or "**it sounds funny.**" Named and condemned. If you cannot state the reason, don't make the change.
- **Anti-pattern — rigidity**: the book quotes the judgment that if the worst disease in copyediting is arrogance toward authors, the second worst is rigidity. Editors are not hired to defend the language against innovation. Aim between permissiveness and pedantry.
- **Illusory knowledge** *(term)*: what you *think* you know but don't. Subject familiarity helps you catch real errors, but it is also the mechanism by which editors confidently introduce new ones.
- **Verification stopping rule**: *before correcting a presumed error, look it up* — but do not "dive down that rabbit hole of Googling every statement of fact." For factual problems, **query the author and let them do the research.** Copyeditors in book and corporate publishing are not normally responsible for factual correctness (journalism differs).
- **Absolute limit on content editing**: never alter meaning because you disagree with the author or believe they could not have meant what they said. The recourse is always to point out the difficulty and ask.

### Part 1, Ch. 1 — Levels of Copyediting

- **Core idea**: Intervention intensity is a *decision made in advance*, driven as much by budget, schedule, audience, and the author's temperament as by the quality of the writing.
- **Light–Medium–Heavy grid** — the standard scheme. Roughly:
  - *Light*: enforce mechanical consistency; correct only indisputable errors of grammar/syntax/usage; **point out** wordy or convoluted patches but do not revise; query factual inconsistencies.
  - *Medium*: correct all errors of grammar/syntax/usage; point out or revise infelicities; **supply suggested revisions** for convoluted patches; verify content against standard references; query faulty organization and gaps in logic.
  - *Heavy*: correct errors *and infelicities*; **rewrite** wordy or convoluted patches; verify and revise incorrect facts; fix faulty organization and logic gaps.
  - Note the real distinction across levels is not effort but **who does the rewriting** — at Light the author does, at Heavy the editor does.
- **FIQ checklist** — an alternative scheme itemizing each problem type as **F**ix, **I**gnore, or **Q**uery, keyed to audience or publication type. In the book's worked table, the same issue routes differently by audience: minor grammar errors are *ignored* for in-house readers but *fixed* for public-facing copy; wordiness is *ignored* in-house, *queried* for high-visibility clients, *fixed* for the general public. **How**: build a small FIQ table for the job before starting; it converts vague "level" talk into per-category instructions.
- **Stages-of-review scheme** — a third grid assigning principal vs. secondary emphasis by stage: early draft = audience suitability, organization, coherence; final draft = pacing, wordiness, punctuation, grammar; final copy = grammar and spelling only.
- **Assessment questions before choosing a level**: Who is the primary audience? How much do they know? Will they read straight through or consult sections? Is this a keepsake or a skim-once document? Note the modern addition of the **global audience** — readers with limited English proficiency, unfamiliar with Anglo-American references, with particular cultural sensitivities.

### Part 1, Ch. 1 — Editorial Triage

- **Core idea**: When the deadline is impossible, do not simply start at page one and run out of time. Set priorities first, and apply them evenly across the whole document.
- **Governing principle**: it is more important to have read every page than to have labored over the first half and barely glanced at the rest.
- **Minimal task list** — correct spelling errors, serious grammatical errors, and egregious punctuation; query factual inconsistencies; ensure all initialisms and acronyms are defined; identify material needing permission; read the title/copyright/contents pages carefully; check numbering of notes, tables, figures. Explicitly *ignored* at this level: mechanical inconsistencies that don't impede communication (capitalization, hyphenation, italics, list formatting) and almost all diction, syntax, and content.
- **If slightly more time**: break up overly long sentences and paragraphs, revise passive-voice overuse, prune repetition and redundancy.
- **Two passes or one?** — a fast double pass, or one slow pass plus a *selective* second pass revisiting only flagged trouble spots. Mark trouble spots on pass one so you can find them.
- **Domain-specific triage** — the priority order is not universal. Technical editing (Tarutz) asks what errors readers will notice and care about, and accepts the asymmetry: fix trivia that's trivially fixable; ignore what bothers you but doesn't bother the reader — *the fact that it bothers you does not make it wrong*. Business writing (Blake) puts organization, tone, and vague language at the top and mechanical errors at the bottom. Legal prose (Garner) works in three passes: clear out the underbrush, make basic sentence-level edits, then refine structure and content if time allows.

### Part 1, Ch. 2 — Querying

- **Core idea**: Querying skill ranks with punctuation and grammar as a core competence. Both over- and under-querying fail, in different ways.
- **The failure modes**: query too often → the author is worn down and stops reading queries; query too seldom → the author stets error-ridden text, misses a meaning change you introduced, or feels you took over the manuscript. Query rudely or confusingly → you don't get the cooperation you need.
- **When to query** — meaning is unclear or ambiguous; you want the author to choose among rewordings; you need to explain a complex revision; you need information rechecked; there's an internal contradiction; the argument has a logical error; you know the topic and have good reason to doubt a fact (with "consummate tact").
- **When *not* to query** — routine mechanical changes not subject to the author's veto. Don't explain every comma or lowercasing. **But**: flag potentially controversial mechanical changes at first occurrence, and *always* query a mechanical change that could alter meaning.
- **Substantive discrepancies that always merit a query**: factual inconsistencies within the document; points you are verifiably certain are wrong; mismatches between evidence presented and the author's interpretation of it; mismatches between text and accompanying figures; missing or incomplete sources.
- **Length and tone** — the best queries are succinct, polite, and specific. `"OK as edited?"` is often enough. **Anti-pattern**: one-word queries like `"Logic?"` — the author can't tell what the problem is and may feel attacked. **Anti-pattern**: long discursive queries — they impose on the author's time far more than you estimate, because responding means rereading the surrounding passage, reconstructing intent, and drafting a fix.
- **Cost model for queries**: every request, however small and however polite, spends the author's time and patience. An accumulation of trivial queries can demoralize an author into abandoning the review entirely. Budget queries as a scarce resource.

### Part 1, Ch. 2 — Style Sheets

- **Style sheet** *(term)*: a running record of every mechanical *decision* made during a job — not a list of every error corrected. Required for any project longer than a few pages.
- **How**: stop each time you make a choice about spelling, capitalization, numbers, abbreviations, or hyphenation, and record it. Record **the rule plus its exceptions** ("Chicago numbers throughout. Exception: 4 Cs") rather than every instance. Novices should over-document until the conventions are internalized.
- **Test for what belongs**: a sentence full of typos generates corrections but *no* style-sheet entries; a sentence with no errors at all may generate four, if it contains four decisions (an acronym's internal punctuation, a proper-name capitalization, a contested plural, a compound's hyphenation).
- **Why it matters downstream**: when the author's revisions come back weeks later, the style sheet is how you keep their additions consistent with the edited text; designers, proofreaders, and indexers consult it too.

### Part 1, Ch. 1 — Professionalism and Ethics

- **Do no harm** — cited as the first principle most editors name, though the book notes it is not from any actual editorial oath and is commonly misattributed to Hippocrates.
- **95 percent accuracy** is about the ceiling of human error-catching; certification tests pass around 80 percent. Accuracy degrades further on error-riddled copy. Perfection is not the standard; consistency of attention is.
- **Plagiarism**: not the editor's job to validate originality, but on discovering irrefutable evidence, the editor is obliged to tell the publisher and recommend proper credit.

### Part 2, Ch. 8 — Quotations *(the chapter that matters most for transcript work)*

- **Core idea**: written and spoken source material are governed by **different rules**, and the difference is exactly the license a transcript editor operates under.
- **The load-bearing distinction**: editors may **never alter quotations from printed sources** to conform to house style — but they **are permitted to *style*, though not to *reword*, speeches and quotations from oral interviews.** This is the formal warrant for cleaning up a transcript at all, and simultaneously its limit.
- **Spoken forms → conventional written forms**: acronyms and abbreviations spoken aloud are rendered as they are conventionally written. The book's example converts a spoken honorific, a spoken ordinal, and a phonetically-rendered technical acronym into their standard written equivalents.
- **Permitted spoken-language interventions** (some publishers encourage or require these): minor corrections to a speaker's grammar; elimination of false starts; deletion of voiced hesitations. Worked instances include repairing subject–verb agreement in a spoken sentence, and cutting a self-correction where the speaker misstates a product number and immediately fixes it.
- **Misspellings in a written source**: innocent typos may be **silently corrected**. Reproduce the original only when the spelling itself is at issue, or in literary/historical analysis where original orthography carries meaning — in which case say so once in a note rather than sprinkling `[sic]` throughout. **Anti-pattern**: repeated `[sic]` reads as snark at the source.
- **Odd wording in a written source**: never revise it. The available moves are (1) let it stand; (2) bracketed `[sic]`; (3) bracketed clarifying words; (4) a note confirming the transcription is accurate; (5) paraphrase instead of quoting.
- **Editor's checklist for any quotation**: query or correct obvious spelling errors; flag odd wording suggesting mistyping or miscopying; be consistent about run-in vs. set-off treatment; check that quotation marks are paired and nested correctly; check that quoted syntax fits the surrounding sentence; mark ellipsis points correctly and delete unnecessary ones; ensure attribution.
- **Brackets** are the designated container for editor-supplied material inside quoted matter.

### Part 3, Chs. 14–15 — Language Editing and Beyond Grammar

- **Grammar vs. usage vs. diction** *(terms)*: grammar is the rule set for combining words (including syntax and morphology); **usage** is the collective habit of native speakers, especially educated ones — and most of what editors actually wrestle with is usage, not grammar; **diction** is simply word choice.
- **Usage is fashion, and fashion turns.** The book's cases: *data* as plural vs. mass noun (defensible either way, decided by field and house style — or dodged by choosing a different word); sentence-adverb *hopefully*, denounced for decades and now largely accepted, with some editors still fighting a war that ended. **How**: when a usage is contested, decide by register, author preference, publisher brand, and likely reader reaction — not by which side is "right."
- **Anti-pattern — deference to outdated authority**: the book explicitly cautions that many precepts in the most-quoted classroom style guide are oversimplified, outdated, or wrong, and that a century of subsequent language change has to be taken into account.
- Beyond grammar, the editor's remit includes organization, point of view, expository style, **plain language compliance**, **accessibility**, **Global English**, **EFL/ESL editing**, **bias-free language**, and awareness of publishing law (libel, privacy, obscenity).
- **Bias-free language** is a normal expectation of the copyeditor's role, not an optional extra: most publishers expect editors to help authors avoid inadvertent sexism and other biased language.

## Worked Example *(one for the whole book)*

**The serial comma that invents a department.** A directive lists the departments it applies to: order fulfillment, customer service, marketing, media and print advertising. House style requires the serial comma, so the mechanical, apparently unarguable edit is to insert a comma before the final *and*.

But doing so silently converts a single "media and print advertising" department into two. The edit is *correct by house style* and *wrong in substance* — and nothing on the page tells the editor which reading is intended.

The resolution is not to pick the more likely reading, and not to leave the sentence alone either. It is to recognize that a mechanical change has crossed into meaning, and therefore to query: should this read "marketing, and media and print advertising," or "marketing, media, and print advertising"? The example is the book's cleanest demonstration of the rule that mechanical changes are exempt from querying **only until they touch meaning** — after which the exemption evaporates entirely, regardless of how routine the change looked.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- If you cannot say *why* a change helps the reader, don't make it. "It looks funny" and "it sounds funny" are not reasons.
- When a mechanical change could alter meaning, query it — the routine-changes exemption does not apply.
- Fix mechanics; **query content.** Never resolve a factual or logical problem by rewriting it into something that reads better.
- Look it up before correcting a presumed error; but do not research every claim — hand factual verification back to the author.
- Set the level before you start, in writing, and derive an FIQ table from it if the categories are contestable.
- Under time pressure, cover the whole document at a lower level rather than perfecting the opening. Announce which lower-priority items you deferred.
- Record decisions, not instances: the rule and its exceptions.
- Declare pervasive changes once, at first occurrence, then apply them silently.
- Prefer "OK as edited?" to a paragraph of justification, and prefer a paragraph of justification to "Logic?"
- Treat commission as worse than omission: a missed error is a blemish; an introduced error is a fault.
- On contested usage, decide by audience and register, not by who is technically correct.
- Never alter meaning because you disagree with the speaker or think they can't have meant it.

## Key Takeaways

1. **"It's not my manuscript."** The editor serves author, publisher, and reader — self-expression is not on the list.
2. **Fix mechanics, query content.** The dividing line is the whole discipline.
3. **Spoken sources may be *styled* but not *reworded*** — the specific license and the specific limit for transcript editing.
4. **Level-setting precedes editing.** Light/Medium/Heavy, or FIQ by category, decided before the first change.
5. **Triage evenly.** Reading every page at a lower level beats perfecting the first third.
6. **A style sheet records decisions, not corrections** — rule plus exceptions.
7. **Queries are expensive.** Each one spends the author's finite patience; spend them on meaning, not on commas.
